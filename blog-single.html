Mergers, Demergers, and Acquisitions: A Practical Guide to Understanding the Core Differences in Egyptian Law.

In Egypt's dynamic business landscape, corporate restructuring processes serve as indispensable strategic tools for achieving growth, expansion, and enhancing competitive capabilities. Although the terms "merger," "demerger," and "acquisition" are often mentioned together, they are not synonymous. Rather, they are distinct legal mechanisms, each with its own unique nature, procedures, and implications. Understanding these differences is not merely an academic exercise; it is the cornerstone of sound strategic decision-making that ensures the achievement of corporate objectives while mitigating legal risks.

These operations are governed by a precise and multi-faceted legislative framework in Egypt, primarily based on Companies Law No. 159 of 1981 and its Executive Regulations, which regulate mergers and demergers

When listed companies are involved, the Capital Market Law No. 95 of 1992 becomes paramount, imposing strict rules on acquisitions to protect investors.

More recently, the Competition Law No. 3 of 2005, as amended by Law No. 175 of 2022, has introduced a new dimension by mandating a pre-merger control system for major transactions.4 This article aims to deconstruct these concepts and present them in a professional and simplified manner.

First: Mergers - A Complete Legal Union.

A merger is a legal process whereby two or more companies are unified into a single legal entity. The defining characteristic of a merger is the dissolution of the legal personality of the merged company, which is fully absorbed into the surviving company.

Mergers take two primary forms:

● Merger by Absorption: In this form, an existing company (the absorbing company) "swallows" another company (the absorbed company), whereby the absorbing company continues to exist while the absorbed company is dissolved.

● Merger by Consolidation: Here, two or more companies are dissolved together to form an entirely new company that did not previously exist.

The most significant legal effect of a merger is universal succession; all assets, liabilities, rights, and contracts of the absorbed company are transferred to the new entity by the force of law, without the need for separate transfer procedures. Merger procedures are distinguished by their formal and regulated nature, requiring corporate approvals from the general assemblies of the involved companies and oversight from the General Authority for Investment and Free Zones (GAFI), which forms specialized committees to evaluate the net assets of the companies to ensure the fairness of the transaction.

Second: Demergers - An Organized Legal Separation.

A demerger is the inverse of a merger. Instead of consolidation, a demerger aims to separate the assets and liabilities of a single company into two or more independent legal entities.

Companies resort to demergers to achieve specific strategic objectives, such as focusing on core activities, isolating high-risk operations, or preparing a part of the company for a future sale.

A demerger results in the creation of new companies, each possessing an independent legal personality upon its registration in the Commercial Registry. Similar to a merger, a demerger requires formal procedures, including the valuation of the demerged assets by specialized committees at GAFI and the approval of the company's extraordinary general assembly.

Third: Acquisitions - The Purchase of Control.

An acquisition is fundamentally different from a merger. It is not a legal union but rather a transaction to purchase a controlling stake in another company with the aim of controlling its management and strategic decisions.

The key distinction here is that the acquired company retains its independent legal personality. What changes is the ownership and control structure, not the legal existence of the company.

The mechanics of an acquisition vary significantly based on the nature of the target company:

● For Non-Listed Companies: The transaction is typically conducted through direct negotiations and private contracts, most notably a Share Purchase Agreement (SPA). The Due Diligence phase is the cornerstone of these deals, where the buyer conducts a comprehensive review of all legal and financial aspects of the target company before completing the purchase.

● For Listed Companies: The process is subject to strict supervision by the Financial Regulatory Authority (FRA) and the rules of the Capital Market Law. To protect minority shareholders, the law imposes a mechanism of Mandatory Tender Offers (MTOs), which obligates an acquirer whose ownership exceeds a certain threshold (typically one-third of the capital) to extend an offer to purchase the shares of all other shareholders at the same price and terms, thereby providing them with a fair exit opportunity.

The Most Significant Development: The Pre-Transaction Control System.

The latest amendment to the Competition Law, which came into effect on June 1, 2024, marks a paradigm shift in regulating these transactions.Under this new system, it is prohibited to close any merger or acquisition that exceeds certain financial thresholds without obtaining prior approval from the Egyptian Competition Authority (ECA).

A pre-notification is required if the combined annual turnover in Egypt of the parties involved exceeds EGP 900 million (provided that the turnover of at least two of the parties each exceeds EGP 200 million), or if their worldwide turnover exceeds EGP 7.5 billion (provided that the turnover in Egypt of at least one party exceeds EGP 200 million). This amendment has added a new regulatory layer and a mandatory waiting period for major transactions, necessitating early strategic and legal planning.

Practical Comparison and Strategic Considerations.

When choosing between these mechanisms, several key factors must be considered:

● Tax Implications: Mergers and demergers offer a significant advantage, which is the possibility of deferring capital gains tax arising from the transaction, subject to specific conditions under the Income Tax Law. In contrast, an acquisition is typically subject to immediate taxation.

● Employee Rights: In a merger, employee contracts are automatically transferred to the new entity by the force of Labor Law No. 12 of 2003, and the new entity is jointly and severally liable for all their rights. In a share acquisition, employment contracts remain with the target company, as its legal personality does not change.

● Procedures and Speed: An acquisition (especially of a non-listed company) is generally the faster and more flexible option as it relies on a contractual agreement. In contrast, mergers and demergers involve longer and more complex institutional procedures due to the requirement for official valuations and approvals from GAFI.

Conclusion: Which Path is Right for Your Company?

The choice between a merger, demerger, or acquisition is primarily a strategic decision.

● Choose a merger if your goal is complete integration and operational unity, while also benefiting from tax advantages.

● Choose a demerger if you are seeking internal restructuring, focusing on specific business sectors, or isolating risks.

● Choose an acquisition if your objective is swift and flexible control without the complexities of a full legal consolidation.

In all cases, and especially in light of the recent legislative developments, engaging specialized legal counsel has become more critical than ever to ensure full compliance with the law and to successfully achieve your strategic objectives safely and effectively.
