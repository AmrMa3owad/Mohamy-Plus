Mergers & Acquisitions under Egyptian Law

Mergers, Demergers, and Acquisitions: A Practical Guide to Understanding the Core Differences in Egyptian Law.

In Egypt's dynamic business landscape, corporate restructuring processes serve as indispensable strategic tools for achieving growth, expansion, and enhancing competitive capabilities. Although the terms "merger," "demerger," and "acquisition" are often mentioned together, they are not synonymous. Rather, they are distinct legal mechanisms, each with its own unique nature, procedures, and implications. Understanding these differences is not merely an academic exercise; it is the cornerstone of sound strategic decision-making that ensures the achievement of corporate objectives while mitigating legal risks.

These operations are governed by a precise and multi-faceted legislative framework in Egypt, primarily based on Companies Law No. 159 of 1981 and its Executive Regulations, which regulate mergers and demergers

When listed companies are involved, the Capital Market Law No. 95 of 1992 becomes paramount, imposing strict rules on acquisitions to protect investors.

More recently, the Competition Law No. 3 of 2005, as amended by Law No. 175 of 2022, has introduced a new dimension by mandating a pre-merger control system for major transactions. This article aims to deconstruct these concepts and present them in a professional and simplified manner.

First: Mergers - A Complete Legal Union.

A merger is a legal process whereby two or more companies are unified into a single legal entity. The defining characteristic of a merger is the dissolution of the legal personality of the merged company, which is fully absorbed into the surviving company.

Mergers commonly take one of two forms:

  • Merger by absorption: One company absorbs another. The absorbing company continues to exist, while the absorbed company is dissolved.
  • Merger by consolidation: Two or more companies are dissolved to form a new company.

A merger may involve universal succession, but the effect on each asset, liability, contract, and required registration depends on the applicable law and transaction structure. Formal approvals and regulatory steps may also apply, including procedures involving the General Authority for Investment and Free Zones (GAFI).

Second: Demergers - An Organized Legal Separation.

A demerger is the inverse of a merger. Instead of consolidation, a demerger aims to separate the assets and liabilities of a single company into two or more independent legal entities.

Companies resort to demergers to achieve specific strategic objectives, such as focusing on core activities, isolating high-risk operations, or preparing a part of the company for a future sale.

A demerger results in the creation of new companies, each possessing an independent legal personality upon its registration in the Commercial Registry. Similar to a merger, a demerger requires formal procedures, including the valuation of the demerged assets by specialized committees at GAFI and the approval of the company's extraordinary general assembly.

Third: Acquisitions - The Purchase of Control.

An acquisition is fundamentally different from a merger. It is not a legal union but rather a transaction to purchase a controlling stake in another company with the aim of controlling its management and strategic decisions.

The key distinction here is that the acquired company retains its independent legal personality. What changes is the ownership and control structure, not the legal existence of the company.

The process depends on whether the target company is listed:

  • Unlisted companies: Transactions commonly use negotiated contracts such as a share purchase agreement. Legal and financial due diligence helps the parties assess the target before completion.
  • Listed companies: Financial Regulatory Authority (FRA) rules include a mandatory tender offer trigger when an acquirer reaches one-third or more of a target's capital or voting rights. Additional triggers and exceptions may apply, so confirm the current rules against the transaction facts.

The Most Significant Development: The Pre-Transaction Control System.

Egypt's ex-ante merger-control regime took effect on June 1, 2024. An in-scope economic concentration that meets the applicable notification thresholds must be notified to the Egyptian Competition Authority (ECA) before implementation.

The domestic threshold includes combined Egyptian annual turnover or assets above EGP 900 million, together with at least two concerned persons each having Egyptian annual turnover above EGP 200 million. The international threshold includes worldwide combined annual turnover or assets above EGP 7.5 billion, together with Egyptian annual turnover above EGP 200 million for the target. These figures are a summary: the definitions of concerned persons, calculation rules, and applicable exceptions affect whether a filing is required. Check current ECA guidance before signing or implementing a transaction.

Practical Comparison and Strategic Considerations.

Compare the legal process, timing, tax treatment, and effect on employees before choosing a structure:

  • Tax: Tax consequences depend on the structure and facts. Specific tax deferral treatment may be available under statutory conditions; review the proposal with Egyptian tax counsel.
  • Employment: Effects differ between mergers, demergers, and share acquisitions. Confirm employee rights, transfer requirements, and liabilities under current law.
  • Timing and approvals: Contractual acquisitions may involve fewer corporate steps, while mergers and demergers can require additional valuations and regulatory approvals.

Conclusion: Which Path is Right for Your Company?

The right structure depends on the company's objectives, approvals, and risk profile:

  • Merger: Consider when the parties seek closer integration, after assessing legal, regulatory, and tax effects.
  • Demergers: Consider when restructuring operations or separating business lines is a priority.
  • Acquisitions: Consider when the intended outcome is a change in ownership or control without combining legal entities.

Transaction-specific advice should account for corporate, competition, securities, tax, employment, and sector-specific rules. This overview is general information, not legal advice; consult qualified Egyptian counsel about the facts and current law before acting.
